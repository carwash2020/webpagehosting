Dryer Running But Not Heating? Here's the Part That's Almost Always Dead

The drum spins, the timer counts down, and forty-five minutes later everything's still damp. Before you start pricing new dryers, check one part that costs less than a movie ticket.

We get some version of this call two or three times a week: "My dryer still spins, but nothing's drying anymore." Sometimes people describe it as running colder than it used to. Sometimes it's a load that used to take forty minutes now going ninety with no results. Almost every time, it's the same eighteen-dollar part.

It's called a thermal fuse, and it sits on the blower housing. That's the metal box at the back of the machine that the exhaust air passes through. Its whole job is to protect the dryer from overheating. Once it does its job, it's done for good. It's not a breaker you can reset. It's a one-time-use fuse, and if it's blown, the heating element gets no power at all, even though the motor and drum keep running just fine.

How to know it's actually this, and not something bigger

The tell is pretty specific. If your dryer:

  • Still tumbles and runs a normal cycle length
  • Makes normal drum and motor noise
  • Comes out at the end with clothes that are damp, or only slightly warm instead of hot

...that's a thermal fuse in probably eight or nine cases out of ten. If the drum isn't turning at all, or you're hearing grinding or squealing, that's a different problem entirely: a belt or a bearing, not this.

What we actually replace

On Whirlpool-built dryers (that covers Whirlpool, Maytag, Kenmore, KitchenAid, and Roper, since they all come off the same lines), it's almost always part WP3392519. Fifteen to twenty dollars in parts. The labor is the harder part, since it means pulling the front or back panel to get at the housing.

Why it blew in the first place

Here's the thing nobody tells you when you just swap the fuse and move on: something usually made it blow. Almost always, it's a restricted exhaust vent. Lint builds up somewhere in the duct run, behind the machine, at a joint, or right at the outside vent flap, and the dryer starts running hotter than it's supposed to, because the hot air it's pushing out has nowhere to go fast enough.

If you just replace the fuse and never touch the vent, you're buying yourself a few weeks or months before it blows again. We always check the full vent run when we're already back there. It's ten extra minutes, and it's the difference between a fix that lasts and a repeat call.

Is it worth fixing, or is the dryer just old?

For this specific problem, almost always worth fixing, regardless of the dryer's age. A blown thermal fuse isn't a sign the machine is dying. It's a sign the vent needs cleaning, which is true of a ten-year-old dryer and a two-year-old one alike. We'd only start talking replacement if you're also seeing other issues stack up on top of it: a drum that's started squealing, a control board acting up, that kind of thing.

Dryer not heating, or not sure what you're looking at? We can usually get it diagnosed and fixed same visit.
